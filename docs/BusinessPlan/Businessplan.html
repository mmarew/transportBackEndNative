01 · Overview

Executive summary

Dynamics Transport Tech is building an app that connects freight vehicle owners and drivers with goods-transport jobs. The platform is ready to begin operations, but the business is pre-revenue and pre-pilot. This plan is therefore about proving demand before spending to scale.

The proposed approach is a three-month discovery period in Addis Ababa with 10 transport companies that each operate more than 100 vehicles, followed by a six-month fleet pilot that puts their full fleets on the platform and reaches out to the shippers they already serve. More companies join from month 4. Djibouti is added in month 10 using the same fleet-first playbook, alongside an operations team of call-centre operators and support staff. The proposed 2% commission is a hypothesis to test, not a validated price.

Status: pre-pilot, not investor-ready. There are no paying customers, completed-job records, verified market size, or funded pilot budget yet. The purpose of this version is to define what must be proven and the gates for spending more money.
Funding request: not yet determined. The next raise should fund discovery and the pilot. Amount, valuation, and terms should follow the month-3 and month-6 evidence gates, not precede them.

What this plan is

A pre-pilot decision document. It defines the customer problem, the pilot design, the evidence required, the gates for spending more money, and the scenario model that shows what the current assumptions imply.

What this plan is not

It is not a demand forecast, a completed market-size study, or a fundable investment case yet. It does not prove that drivers and shippers will use the app, or that the 2% commission can be collected.

02 · Business

Customers, launch sequence and go-to-market

Service concept

The app connects companies that need goods moved with drivers and vehicle owners who have capacity. The platform records job posting, matching, and completion. Current scope is the driver/job flow; other roles and features still need definition. Success depends on liquidity on both sides, not just software.

Customer and value

Supply is freight vehicle owners, fleet-owning transport companies, and drivers. Demand is goods-owning companies, traders, and brokers. The proposed value is faster matching, fewer empty trips, clearer pricing, and transaction records. Each of these is a hypothesis to test with customers.

Launch sequence (Addis Ababa first)

  1. Months 1–3 · DiscoveryInterview and test 10 transport companies that each operate more than 100 vehicles, and speak to the drivers and shippers they work with. Map competitors and fees, secure 3–5 letters of intent, and define pilot success metrics. No scale spending.
  2. Months 4–6 · Fleet pilotOnboard the full fleets of the 10 companies — more than 1,000 vehicles — and reach out to the shippers those companies already serve, so their existing traffic becomes platform demand. Match jobs manually where needed, assist onboarding, and test commission collection and repeat usage.
  3. Months 7–9 · Productise and measureTrack retention, acquisition cost, support cost per job, and off-platform leakage. In parallel, prepare Djibouti: local partner, customs and permits, payment route, and the first fleet cohort.
  4. Month 10 · Djibouti entry and Addis expansionLaunch Djibouti with the same fleet-first playbook and a limited first cohort, while continuing to expand in Addis. Recruit the operations layer — call-centre operators, customer support, and a Djibouti coordinator — and move those salaries into the month-10 budget.
  5. Months 11–24 · Scale both marketsAdd routes, segments and fleets one at a time in each market. Djibouti is a separate operating plan with its own costs, not an extension of the Addis budget.
Anchor-fleet approach: the 10 discovery companies operate more than 1,000 vehicles between them. Because each of these companies already runs more than 100 vehicles, the pilot can run their full fleets instead of recruiting driver by driver, and can draw demand from the shippers those fleets already serve. Discovery therefore has to test whether they will list capacity, accept the 2% commission, and route jobs through the platform. Anchor fleets are the fastest route to liquidity, and also the fastest way to lose it if those companies prefer to keep jobs in-house.

Cold-start and operations plan

Supply side

Bring the anchor fleets on company by company, so each one's full fleet is live before the next is added. Fill capacity gaps in dense clusters such as truck parks, fuel stations, and broker networks. Use assisted onboarding and phone/WhatsApp fallback for low-data users. Verify vehicles and drivers, and commit to response times so drivers see reliable work.

Demand side

Reach out to the shippers each fleet already serves, and convert them to platform demand with committed volumes or letters of intent rather than open sign-ups. Focus on one route or cargo type, hand-match the first jobs, and provide account management. Reliability in a narrow niche matters more than broad coverage at launch.

Transaction and commission collection

The plan assumes a 2% commission on completed jobs. The key operating question is who pays, when, and whether the transaction stays on the platform. Once a driver and shipper meet, they can transact directly and avoid the fee. Proposed mitigations are digital receipts, ratings, dispute handling, shipper contracts, prepaid or escrow options, and value-added services. These must be tested in the pilot; the plan does not yet prove that leakage can be controlled.

Published market context

  • Large, cross-border population base. World Bank estimates put Ethiopia's population at about 132.1 million in 2024 and Djibouti's at about 1.17 million in 2024. These figures describe overall population, not the number of drivers, shippers, or potential app customers. Ethiopia population data · Djibouti population data [1]
  • Material Ethiopia–Djibouti freight corridor. The World Bank reported in 2023 that over 95% of Ethiopia's import-export trade by volume uses the Addis–Djibouti corridor. Its corridor project targets regional connectivity and logistics efficiency and identifies growing truck traffic. This supports the strategic relevance of freight movement on the corridor, but does not establish how many loads the app can capture. [2]
  • Digital reach needs validation. World Bank data reports that 20.3% of Ethiopia's population used the internet in 2023. This is a national population measure, not smartphone ownership or connectivity among commercial drivers. It makes offline/low-data access and assisted onboarding important assumptions to test. [3]
These are context indicators, not a market-size estimate. The 500 monthly vehicle registrations, 40% payer rate, one job per paying vehicle per month, and ETB 200,000 average job value remain business-plan assumptions; the sources above do not verify them.

Evidence still needed for an investor case

  • Build a bottom-up estimate of addressable freight vehicles and shippers in the launch corridor; document the method and source.
  • Summarise interviews with the 10 transport companies that each operate more than 100 vehicles, covering current job sourcing, unmet needs, willingness to use the app, and willingness to pay a 2% commission.
  • Document competitor and informal-channel coverage, fees, and service differences based on the planned market research.
  • Run the Ethiopia pilot and report registrations, active vehicles, completed jobs, repeat usage, payer conversion, average job value, and commission collection before projecting these as observed outcomes.
  • Confirm Djibouti customs, permits, payments, partner and operating costs during months 7–9, before the month-10 entry.

The base case assumes 500 registrations per month from month 4, 40% payer conversion after a six-month free period, one job per paying vehicle per month, ETB 200,000 average job value, and a 2% commission. The pilot runs the 10 companies' full fleets in Addis. The 24-month table excludes Djibouti revenue and the month-10 operations headcount, so it understates both revenue and cost after month 10.

  • Registrations: 500 vehicles per month across the addressable market in the scenario. The pilot's 10 companies alone operate more than 1,000 vehicles, roughly two months of that rate, so the assumption depends on converting a handful of large fleets, not on mass driver-by-driver sign-ups.
  • Free period: six months per vehicle cohort. The first month-4 cohort is modelled as paying from month 10.
  • Conversion and activity: 40% of each cohort pays after the free period and completes one job per month.
  • Revenue: 2% commission on completed jobs. Company annual fees and other revenue streams are excluded.

What must be true

  • Demand density: shippers post enough loads to keep registered vehicles utilised.
  • Reliable supply: drivers complete jobs reliably and stay active after onboarding.
  • On-platform transactions: completed jobs are recorded and the commission is collectible without unacceptable leakage.
  • Conversion: a meaningful share of free users become paying users after the free period.
  • Unit economics: acquisition and support cost per job are below commission per job.
  • Cross-border readiness: the Djibouti partner, permits, payment route, and first fleet cohort are in place before month 10.

04 · Competition

Competition, alternatives and moat

The main competitor is not another app. It is the informal broker/phone network that already moves most freight. The plan must beat the existing way of doing business on speed, reliability, price transparency, or trust—not merely by digitising it.

Alternative Why customers use it Proposed response
Informal brokers and phone dispatch Relationships, cash payment, flexibility. Verified capacity, receipts, response-time commitments, and dispute handling.
Existing transport and logistics companies Assets, contracts, and operating experience. Asset-light marketplace with broader shipper reach and transparent pricing.
Digital load boards and WhatsApp groups Low cost and familiar behaviour. Verification, ratings, and payment/collection layer that informal channels lack.
Direct shipper–driver relationships Cheapest option when capacity is available. Backup capacity and reliability when their usual vehicle is unavailable.

Proposed moat

  • Local liquidity: density in one corridor beats broad coverage across many.
  • Anchor fleet agreements: contracted capacity from companies that already run more than 100 vehicles each, which builds liquidity faster than driver-by-driver recruitment.
  • Verified capacity: driver and vehicle checks plus reliability history.
  • Trust data: transaction records, ratings, and dispute outcomes that reduce risk for both sides.
  • Collection infrastructure: escrow, prepaid, or shipper contracts that make paying easier than avoiding the fee.
  • Assisted onboarding: a local operating layer for users who are not comfortable with self-service apps.
Technology alone is not a moat. The defensible asset is liquidity plus trust data in a specific corridor. Competitor names, coverage, fees, and service levels must be documented in discovery; this draft does not name them because it has not verified them.

08 · Risks

Risks, assumptions and decision gates

The purpose of the pilot is to test these risks, not to prove the plan right. The decision-gate thresholds are proposed and should be confirmed with the pilot budget.

  • Liquidity and chicken-and-egg: the fleets' vehicles sit idle without enough shipper demand, and shippers leave without reliable capacity. Mitigation: start from the fleets' existing shipper relationships, stay in a narrow corridor, and hand-match early jobs.
  • Disintermediation: drivers and shippers can transact directly after meeting. Mitigation: receipts, ratings, contracts, escrow/prepaid options, and services that are easier to use on-platform.
  • Anchor-fleet concentration: a few large fleets could control most of the pilot's supply, press for a lower commission, or route jobs around the platform. Mitigation: contract terms agreed in discovery, a pipeline wider than the first 10 companies, and value that lies with shippers as well as fleets.
  • Low digital adoption: many drivers may not use smartphone apps comfortably. Mitigation: assisted onboarding, WhatsApp/phone fallback, and simple offline-compatible records.
  • Collection and payments: commission may be hard to collect in a cash market. Mitigation: test prepaid, escrow, or shipper-paid models during the pilot.
  • Cost of the free period: six months of free service across 1,000+ vehicles creates unmodelled support and onboarding costs. Mitigation: agree fleet-level terms on volume and support, shorten the free period for fleets if needed, and charge for add-on services.
  • Cross-border complexity: Djibouti adds customs, permits, payments, language, and regulatory work. Mitigation: use months 7–9 to confirm each item and sign a local partner, then enter with a limited first cohort rather than moving the Addis playbook across unchanged.
  • Informal competition: brokers can undercut or copy the service. Mitigation: compete on reliability, verification, and records rather than price alone.
  • Regulatory and insurance risk: licensing, liability, and data rules need confirmation. Mitigation: complete the compliance checklist before live operations.
  • Key-person and team risk: execution depends on a small team. Mitigation: appoint an operations/support lead and document processes before the pilot.

Decision gates and proposed kill criteria

  • Month 3: 10 transport companies interviewed and tested, 3–5 letters of intent, a competitor/fee map, and agreed pilot metrics. If these are not met, do not start paid operations.
  • Month 6: all 10 fleets live with 1,000+ vehicles onboarded, 250+ vehicles with at least one completed job, 30+ active shippers, and 500+ completed jobs, with measured job value, collection rate, and leakage. If not, iterate or stop.
  • Month 9: repeat job rate, payer conversion, acquisition cost, and support cost per job. Do not scale Addis if acquisition cost exceeds commission per job or repeat usage is weak. Djibouti readiness (partner, permits, payments) must also be signed off in month 9.
  • Month 10: Djibouti entry starts only with a signed local partner, confirmed customs and payment route, and a committed first fleet cohort. If readiness slips, delay Djibouti but keep Addis expansion running.
  • Month 18: decide how far to scale Djibouti from cross-border job volume and contribution per job, not from the Addis result alone.

09 · Funding and readiness

Funding, milestones and investor readiness

Funding should be staged behind evidence, not raised as a single 24-month commitment. The first raise funds discovery and the pilot; the next raise is released only if the pilot shows demand, collection, and retention.

Proposed funding stages

  1. Pilot round · months 1–6Fund discovery, the fleet pilot across the 10 companies, and the team required to onboard 1,000+ vehicles and measure demand and collection. Amount to be set from a bottom-up pilot budget.
  2. Scale round · after month-6/9 gatesFund Addis Ababa expansion and the month-10 operations team. Amount depends on measured acquisition cost, support cost, and contribution per job.
  3. Djibouti round · month 10Fund the Djibouti entry: local partner, coordinator, office, compliance, and the first fleet cohort. Sized from the month-9 readiness work, not from a percentage of the Addis budget.
  4. Later rounds · months 12+Scale whichever market shows repeatable demand and positive contribution per job.

Use of funds to price before raising

  • Team and pilot operations.
  • Fleet partnership, driver acquisition, verification, training, and retention.
  • Shipper acquisition and account management.
  • Product, cloud, SMS/data, and mapping costs.
  • Marketing and incentives after the initial promotion.
  • Payment and finance operations, working capital, legal, compliance, and insurance.
  • Month-10 operations: call-centre operators, support staff, fleet account managers, and the Djibouti coordinator.
  • Djibouti entry: local partner, permits, office, travel, and cross-border compliance.
Investor request: not yet determined. Amount, valuation, and terms are intentionally not set. The next deliverables are a pilot budget and an evidence pack from months 1–6.

Investor readiness checklist

  • Evidence pack: interviews, letters of intent, competitor/fee map, and bottom-up market sizing.
  • Pilot results: registrations, active vehicles, completed jobs, repeat usage, payer conversion, average job value, collection rate, acquisition cost, and support cost.
  • Financial model: full cost stack, scenarios, sensitivity, working capital, and cash requirement.
  • Funding ask: amount, use of funds, milestones, valuation, and terms.
  • Team: named roles, bios, gaps, and advisors.
  • Djibouti plan: partner agreement, permits, payment route, local costs, and the first fleet cohort behind the month-10 entry.
  • Legal and regulatory: licensing, insurance, payments, data protection, and cross-border requirements.
  • Risk register and kill criteria (section 08).

This is a pre-pilot working draft. It is deliberately conservative about what is known: the platform exists, but demand, pricing, retention, and collection are unproven. The next version should replace assumptions with measured pilot results.
