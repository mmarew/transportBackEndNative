01 · Overview

Executive summary

Dynamics Transport Tech connects freight vehicle owners and drivers with goods-transport jobs. The platform is built and ready to operate. The company is pre-revenue, so this plan is the case for funding the pilot that produces the first measured demand, collection and retention data.

The sequence is fixed and gated. Three months of discovery with 10 Addis Ababa transport companies that each run more than 100 vehicles. A three-month fleet pilot in months 4–6 that puts the fleets that sign at that gate — up to 1,000+ vehicles if all 10 convert — and the shippers they already serve on the platform. Months 7–9 to measure retention, acquisition cost and leakage while preparing the Djibouti entry, which follows in month 10 on the same fleet-first playbook. The 2% commission is the price that pilot tests, and the operations team needed to run it is hired at each gate rather than at launch.

The ask: ETB 7.957M to fund the pilot through month 9, pension costs, a 12-month senior developer and the full reserve. Tranche 1, ETB 4,163,200 at close: ETB 2,095,000 setup and equipment, ETB 885,000 core operations for months 1–3 excluding the senior developer, ETB 105,000 for the operations lead, ETB 900,000 reserved for 12 months of senior developer salary at ETB 75,000/month, plus ETB 99,000 employer pension for that full year and ETB 79,200 employer pension for months 1–3. Tranche 2 totals ETB 1,287,870, including ETB 100,870 employer pension for months 4–6, and is called only if the month-3 gate passes. Tranche 3 of ETB 1,505,430 funds months 7–9 excluding the senior developer's salary and pension, which were reserved in tranche 1; it is called only if the month-6 gate passes. A further ETB 1,000,000 is raised as an unallocated reserve. The scale round follows the month-9 results.

What the pilot round buys

The anchor fleets that sign at the month-3 gate — up to 10 companies and 1,000+ vehicles — live in one corridor, with demand drawn from the shippers they already serve. Volume inputs are then re-based on the signed cohort, while job value, payer conversion, collection rate, leakage, and acquisition and support cost per job are measured in the pilot rather than assumed.

What that evidence must show

That fleets list capacity, shippers route jobs through the platform, the commission is collectible, and acquisition plus support cost per job stays below commission per job. The thresholds are fixed in section 08.

02 · Business

Customers, launch sequence and go-to-market

Service concept

The app connects companies that need goods moved with drivers and vehicle owners who have capacity. It records job posting, matching and completion. Current scope is the driver/job flow; other roles still need definition. Success depends on liquidity on both sides, not just software.

Customer and value

Supply is freight vehicle owners, fleet-owning transport companies and drivers. Demand is goods-owning companies, traders and brokers. The proposed value is faster matching, fewer empty trips, clearer pricing and transaction records — the claims the pilot tests.

Launch sequence (Addis Ababa first)

  1. Months 1–3 · DiscoveryInterview and test 10 transport companies that each operate more than 100 vehicles, and speak to the drivers and shippers they work with. Map competitors and fees, secure letters of intent, and agree pilot success metrics. No scale spending; thresholds for this gate are in section 08.
  2. Months 4–6 · Fleet pilotOnboard the full fleets of the companies that signed at the month-3 gate — up to 1,000+ vehicles if all 10 convert, 300–500+ if the cohort is the 3–5 company target — and reach out to the shippers those companies already serve, so their existing traffic becomes platform demand. Match jobs manually where needed, assist onboarding, and test commission collection and repeat usage. Further companies come from the pipeline, fleet by fleet, and the forecast is re-based on the signed cohort before this month starts.
  3. Months 7–9 · Productise and measureTrack retention, acquisition cost, support cost per job, and off-platform leakage. In parallel, prepare Djibouti: local partner, customs and permits, payment route, and the first fleet cohort.
  4. Month 10 · Djibouti entry and Addis expansionLaunch Djibouti with the same fleet-first playbook and a limited first cohort, while continuing to expand in Addis. Recruit the operations layer costed in section 06.
  5. Months 11–24 · Scale both marketsAdd routes, segments and fleets one at a time in each market. Djibouti is a separate operating plan with its own costs, not an extension of the Addis budget.
Anchor-fleet approach: the 10 discovery companies operate more than 1,000 vehicles between them. Anchor fleets are the fastest route to liquidity, because the pilot can run the full fleets that sign instead of recruiting driver by driver. They are also the fastest way to lose it, if those companies prefer to keep jobs in-house. Discovery therefore has to test whether they will list capacity, accept the commission, and route jobs through the platform.

Cold-start and operations plan

Supply side

Bring the anchor fleets on company by company, so each one's full fleet is live before the next is added. Fill capacity gaps in dense clusters such as truck parks, fuel stations and broker networks. Verify vehicles and drivers, and commit to response times so drivers see reliable work.

Demand side

Reach out to the shippers each fleet already serves, and convert them to platform demand with committed volumes or letters of intent rather than open sign-ups. Stay on one route or cargo type, and provide account management. Reliability in a narrow niche matters more than broad coverage at launch.

Transaction and commission collection

The plan assumes a 2% commission on completed jobs. The operating question is who pays, when, and whether the transaction stays on the platform: once a driver and shipper meet, they can transact directly and avoid the fee. Proposed mitigations are digital receipts, ratings, dispute handling, shipper contracts, prepaid or escrow options, and value-added services. Proving that leakage can be controlled is the pilot's central test, and it is carried as a risk in section 08.

Published market context

  • Large, cross-border population base. World Bank estimates put Ethiopia's population at about 132.1 million in 2024 and Djibouti's at about 1.17 million in 2024. These figures describe overall population, not the number of drivers, shippers, or potential app customers. Ethiopia population data · Djibouti population data [1]
  • Material Ethiopia–Djibouti freight corridor. The World Bank reported in 2023 that over 95% of Ethiopia's import-export trade by volume uses the Addis–Djibouti corridor. Its corridor project targets regional connectivity and logistics efficiency and identifies growing truck traffic. This supports the strategic relevance of freight movement on the corridor, but does not establish how many loads the app can capture. [2]
  • Digital reach needs validation. World Bank data reports that 20.3% of Ethiopia's population used the internet in 2023. This is a national population measure, not smartphone ownership or connectivity among commercial drivers. It makes offline/low-data access and assisted onboarding important assumptions to test. [3]
These are context indicators, not a market-size estimate. They do not verify any business assumption in this plan; the scenario inputs they sit next to are listed once in section 03.

Evidence this round produces

  • A bottom-up estimate of addressable freight vehicles and shippers in the launch corridor, with the method and sources documented.
  • Interviews with the 10 transport companies, covering current job sourcing, unmet needs, willingness to use the app, and willingness to pay a 2% commission, plus a competitor and informal-channel map of coverage, fees and service differences.
  • Pilot results: registrations, active vehicles, completed jobs, repeat usage, payer conversion, average job value, collection rate, acquisition cost and support cost.
  • Djibouti readiness: partner, customs, permits, payment route and local operating costs confirmed before the month-10 gate.

What must be true

  • Demand density: shippers post enough loads to keep registered vehicles utilised.
  • Reliable supply: drivers complete jobs reliably and stay active after onboarding.
  • On-platform transactions: completed jobs are recorded and the commission is collectible without unacceptable leakage.
  • Conversion: a meaningful share of free users become paying users after the free period.
  • Unit economics: acquisition and support cost per job are below commission per job.

04 · Competition

Competition, alternatives and moat

The main competitor is not another app. It is the informal broker/phone network that already moves most freight. The plan must beat the existing way of doing business on speed, reliability, price transparency, or trust—not merely by digitising it.

Alternative Why customers use it Proposed response
Informal brokers and phone dispatch Relationships, cash payment, flexibility. Verified capacity, receipts, response-time commitments, and dispute handling.
Existing transport and logistics companies Assets, contracts, and operating experience. Asset-light marketplace with broader shipper reach and transparent pricing.
Digital load boards and WhatsApp groups Low cost and familiar behaviour. Verification, ratings, and payment/collection layer that informal channels lack.
Direct shipper–driver relationships Cheapest option when capacity is available. Backup capacity and reliability when their usual vehicle is unavailable.

Proposed moat

The table above lists the product responses. What compounds is structural:

  • Local liquidity: density in one corridor beats broad coverage across many.
  • Anchor fleet agreements: contracted capacity from companies that already run more than 100 vehicles each, which builds liquidity faster than driver-by-driver recruitment.
  • The local operating layer: assisted onboarding, account management and dispute handling for users who are not comfortable with self-service apps.
Technology alone is not a moat. The defensible asset is liquidity plus trust data in a specific corridor. Competitor names, coverage, fees and service levels are documented in discovery as part of the evidence in section 02; this draft does not name them because it has not verified them.

08 · Risks

Risks, assumptions and decision gates

The purpose of the pilot is to test these risks, not to prove the plan right. The gates below are the only thresholds in this document, and the month-3 gate is the one that sets the size of everything after it: the signed cohort determines the fleet count, the registration rate, the revenue rows and the second tranche.

  • Liquidity and chicken-and-egg: the fleets' vehicles sit idle without enough shipper demand, and shippers leave without reliable capacity. Mitigation: the anchor-fleet and shipper-first approach in section 02.
  • Commission leakage: drivers and shippers can meet on the platform and then transact directly, in cash, avoiding the fee. Mitigation: the collection and retention measures in section 02; measured as collection rate and leakage at the month-6 gate.
  • Anchor-fleet concentration: a few large fleets could control most of the pilot's supply, press for a lower commission, or route jobs around the platform. Mitigation: contract terms agreed in discovery, a pipeline wider than the first 10 companies, and value that lies with shippers as well as fleets.
  • Low digital adoption: many drivers may not use smartphone apps comfortably. Mitigation: the assisted onboarding and WhatsApp/phone fallback built into section 02, and simple offline-compatible records.
  • Cost of the free period: six months of free service across the signed cohort (up to 1,000+ vehicles) adds onboarding and support load. Mitigation: fleet-level terms on volume and support, a shorter free period for fleets if needed, add-on charges, and the staffing steps in section 06, which are timed to vehicle count.
  • Cross-border complexity: Djibouti adds customs, permits, payments, language, and regulatory work. Mitigation: use months 7–9 to confirm each item and sign a local partner, then enter with a limited first cohort rather than moving the Addis playbook across unchanged.
  • Informal competition: brokers can undercut or copy the service. Mitigation: compete on reliability, verification, and records rather than price alone.
  • Regulatory and insurance risk: licensing, liability, and data rules need confirmation. Mitigation: the compliance checklist in section 05 is completed before live operations.
  • Key-person and team risk: execution depends on a small team. Mitigation: the operations and support lead is budgeted from month 1 (section 06), and processes are documented before the pilot.

Decision gates and proposed kill criteria

  • Month 3: 10 transport companies interviewed and tested, 3–5 pilot agreements signed (each operating 100+ vehicles, so 300–500+ vehicles committed), a competitor/fee map, agreed pilot metrics, and the signed cohort's vehicle count recorded. The months 4–24 plan — cohort size, registration rate, revenue and the second tranche — is re-based on that count before any pilot spending. If the gate is not met, paid operations do not start.
  • Month 6: every fleet in the signed cohort live, with at least 25% of cohort vehicles carrying one completed job and at least half a completed job per vehicle — 250 vehicles and 500 jobs in the all-10 case — plus 30+ active shippers and measured job value, collection rate and leakage. If not, iterate or stop.
  • Month 9: repeat job rate, payer conversion, acquisition cost, and support cost per job. Do not scale Addis if acquisition cost exceeds commission per job or repeat usage is weak. Djibouti readiness (partner, permits, payments) must also be signed off in month 9.
  • Month 10: Djibouti entry starts only with a signed local partner, confirmed customs and payment route, and a committed first fleet cohort. If readiness slips, delay Djibouti but keep Addis expansion running.
  • Month 18: decide how far to scale Djibouti from cross-border job volume and contribution per job, not from the Addis result alone.

What releases the next round. Month-6 results — job value, collection rate, leakage, payer conversion, and acquisition and support cost per job — against the thresholds in section 08. If they are not met, the plan is to iterate within the pilot rather than raise for scale.

This plan is priced from the owner's cost inputs and settled by one pilot. Valuation and terms are deliberately left to the evidence pack produced by months 1–6, and this document is updated as those results arrive.
